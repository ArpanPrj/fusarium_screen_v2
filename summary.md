# Screening Fusarium DNA and RNA reads for non-host sequences

I examined two public Fusarium graminearum datasets: DNA run SRR27384773 and RNA run SRR1185280. NCBI identifies the DNA sample as Fg207 (strain 207), from a maize ear collected in Brazil in 2011. The RNA sample is PH-1 infected with Fusarium graminearum virus 1 (FgV1), not an unknown infection. Its BioSample is SAMN02673538, under BioProject PRJNA240256. It contains paired-end, poly(A)-selected RNA reads generated on an Illumina HiSeq 2000. The datasets represent different strains and studies, not an infected-versus-uninfected pair.

I used both complete readsets without subsampling: 12,446,790 individual DNA reads and 27,608,126 individual RNA reads. I screened them with Kraken2 using the database already on ASC and SendSketch using online comparisons. I then mapped reads against a combined panel containing the fungal host, virus, bacterial candidates, and humans. Host and candidate references competed for the same reads. I retained primary mapped reads with mapping quality of at least 20 and measured read percentage, average depth, and reference breadth.

The strongest non-host signal was the expected FgV1 component in RNA. A total of 14,497,528 reads (52.511815%) mapped to its 6,624-base reference. All reference bases were covered, with an average depth of approximately 220,615 times. This confirms and quantifies the documented infection; it is not evidence of newly discovered accidental viral contamination. I classify it as biological and also consequential because more than half the input reads represent the virus, affecting the reads available for host analysis. Sample metadata and extensive mapping support favor the infected culture itself as the source over incidental matches or low-level carryover.

Human-reference signals were small: 1,034 DNA reads (0.008307%) and 645 RNA reads (0.002336%), with genome breadths of 0.003989% and 0.001463%. Nine DNA contigs had human-best matches within the panel, supporting a provisional trace assessment. Handling or library carryover is plausible, but shared sequences remain an alternative. The RNA human signal remains unresolved, provisionally classified as noise. Three RNA reads matched Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, which is insufficient for a credible detection. Other tested bacterial references had no retained mapped reads.

I assembled DNA with MEGAHIT and RNA with rnaSPAdes, compared assembled sequences with local BLAST, and made GC-versus-depth plots. Combining alignment regions without double-counting overlaps changed 38 DNA contigs and 2,164 RNA transcripts from partial to broad query support. Read depth was unchanged. Remote BLAST was skipped because it was too slow. Limited reference coverage, capped BLAST results, and ambiguous RNA mappings remain limitations. The main lesson is that a strong non-host signal can be expected sample biology; sample metadata is essential before calling it contamination.

Sources: NCBI BioSamples SAMN02673538 and SAMN39194179; SRA experiment SRX482694; Lee et al. (2014), doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0100989. Links are provided in README.md.
